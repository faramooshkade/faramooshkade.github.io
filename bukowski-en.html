Charles Bukowski

1920 – 1994  |  American poet and novelist of Los Angeles

Who He Was

Bukowski was a German-born American poet and novelist. His family moved to Los Angeles when he was a child, and he spent most of his life there. He wrote about the city's underside: drinking, racetracks, dead-end jobs, cheap rooms, failed romances, and the stubborn urge to write anyway. He published more than 60 books, mostly poetry, and became an icon of outsider literature. Most of his fiction is barely disguised autobiography told through his alter ego, Henry Chinaski.

Why He Is Important

  • A voice for people literature usually ignored: factory hands, mail clerks, drunks, gamblers, the unemployed. He wrote about them from the inside, as one of them, without pity, sentimentality or moralizing. Hardly any serious American writer before him had made the dull, humiliating daily life of low-wage work his main subject.
  • He made literature feel open to everyone: his plain, spoken style showed that poetry and fiction did not have to be academic, decorated or difficult. Many people who never read poetry read Bukowski, and he remains one of the best-selling poets in the world. For many young readers he is the writer who first made books feel alive.
  • Radical honesty: he put his own worst sides on the page — laziness, cruelty, drunkenness, cowardice, failure — and did not make himself look better. This kind of unflinching self-exposure helped open the way for later autobiographical and "autofiction" writing.
  • Success outside the establishment: he rose through little magazines, tiny presses and underground newspapers, not through universities, prizes or big New York publishers. His long partnership with the small Black Sparrow Press showed that an outsider could build a lasting readership on his own terms.
  • The writer of a city: he is to the shabby side of Los Angeles — its bars, racetracks, rented rooms and freeways — what Chandler is to its noir streets. His books are a record of a working-class LA that glamorous images of the city leave out.
  • Influence on culture: he is often placed alongside the "dirty realist" writers like Raymond Carver, and his attitude influenced generations of poets, musicians and filmmakers; singer Tom Waits, for example, recorded a reading of his poem "The Laughing Heart." His image as the anti-literary outsider is part of popular culture.
  • A worldwide readership: translated into dozens of languages, he is especially loved in Germany, France, Italy, Latin America and Iran, where readers often identify with his rage against meaningless work and empty respectability.
  • Hope for late starters: he published his first novel at 51. His life is often cited as proof that it is never too late, and that persistence matters more than credentials.
  • Still argued about: his crudeness and his portrayal of women keep him controversial. That debate is part of why he is still read and discussed, instead of quietly becoming a museum piece.

Life

  • Born Heinrich Karl Bukowski on 16 August 1920 in Andernach, Germany. His father was an American soldier of German descent, his mother a German woman. The family moved to the United States in 1923 and settled in Los Angeles.
  • His childhood was harsh: a strict, violent father who beat him regularly, poverty during the Great Depression, and a severe case of acne that scarred his face and made him an outsider as a teenager. All of this became the material of Ham on Rye.
  • He studied journalism and art for a while at Los Angeles City College, then drifted across America in the 1940s, living in cheap rooms and taking any job he could find.
  • He published his first story at 24, but then gave up writing for nearly ten years and drank heavily. In 1955 a bleeding ulcer almost killed him. After he left the hospital, he began writing poetry.
  • He worked for the U.S. Postal Service, first as a letter carrier and then for many years as a clerk, until 1969.
  • His daughter, Marina, was born in 1964. In the late 1960s his newspaper column Notes of a Dirty Old Man made him an underground celebrity.
  • From 1970, with the support of Black Sparrow Press, he wrote full time. Fame came late, and he was even more popular in Europe, especially Germany, than in America.
  • In 1985 he married Linda Lee Beighle and lived quietly in San Pedro, near Los Angeles. He died of leukemia on 9 March 1994, at 73.

Pulp (1994)

Background

Pulp was Bukowski's last novel. He wrote it in his final years, while he was ill with leukemia, and it came out just after his death in March 1994. It's also the only one of his novels without Chinaski. Bukowski dedicated it "to bad writing," a wink at the cheap "pulp" magazines of the 1930s and 40s, which were printed on rough wood-pulp paper and full of tough detectives, gangsters and femmes fatales.

The story

The narrator is Nick Belane, a broke, hard-drinking and spectacularly incompetent private eye in Los Angeles. He's behind on the rent for his office, he spends his time at the racetrack and in bars, and he's not very good at detecting. His cases get stranger and stranger:

  • Lady Death, a beautiful and terrifying woman, hires him to find the French writer Céline. Céline is supposed to have died in 1961, but he has been seen in an LA bookstore.
  • He's asked to track down the mysterious "Red Sparrow." Nobody can tell him what it is.
  • One case involves an alien who has taken the form of a woman and is controlling a man on Earth.
  • Alongside these, there are ordinary cases too, such as a husband who wants to know if his wife is cheating.

The cases never really get solved in the usual way. They tangle together, and the book ends with Belane finally facing the Red Sparrow.

What it's about

  • Genre parody: it sends up the hardboiled detective novels of Raymond Chandler and Dashiell Hammett, with their tough-guy narrators and LA settings. Belane talks like Philip Marlowe but fails at everything.
  • Death: Bukowski knew he was dying when he wrote it, and Death appears as an actual character. Underneath the jokes, it's a dying man's comedy about death, fate and the pointlessness of the search.
  • The Red Sparrow: many readers see it as a symbol of death or of the meaning that can never be found. The name also echoes Black Sparrow Press, the publisher that supported him for 25 years.
  • Absurdity: aliens and literary ghosts mix with unpaid rent and hangovers, and nobody in the book finds this strange.

Style

  • Plain language: short, blunt sentences with no ornament and no literary showing off. It's often called "dirty realism."
  • Autobiography as fiction: nearly every Chinaski novel is drawn from his own life, often with very little changed. The novels are episodic, made of short chapters rather than a carefully built plot.
  • Dark humor: even his bleakest material is funny, and he mocks himself more than anyone.
  • The anti-hero: his heroes are losers, drunks and bad workers, but honest ones who refuse to pretend.
  • Against the literary world: he distrusted universities, critics and "literary" writing, and wrote for ordinary readers.
  • Criticism: his portrayal of women, especially in Women, has often been criticized as crude and misogynistic, and it remains a topic of debate.

Influences

  • John Fante: discovering Fante's Ask the Dust in the LA public library was a turning point for him. He called Fante his god, and later persuaded Black Sparrow to bring Fante's forgotten books back into print.
  • Louis-Ferdinand Céline: especially Journey to the End of the Night. Making Céline the target of a search in Pulp is a personal tribute.
  • He also admired Hemingway, Dostoevsky, Knut Hamsun (Hunger) and Sherwood Anderson.

Bukowski on Screen

  • Tales of Ordinary Madness (1981), directed by Marco Ferreri, based on his stories.
  • Barfly (1987), directed by Barbet Schroeder, from his own screenplay, starring Mickey Rourke and Faye Dunaway.
  • Factotum (2005), starring Matt Dillon as Chinaski.
  • Bukowski: Born into This (2003), a well-known documentary about his life.

Interesting Facts

  • He started late: at 49, his publisher, John Martin of Black Sparrow Press, offered him $100 a month for life to quit the post office and write full time. He accepted, and wrote Post Office in a matter of weeks.
  • His gravestone says "Don't Try." He meant that you shouldn't force writing, and should wait for it to come.
  • Classical music: he usually wrote at night with a drink beside him and classical music playing on the radio.
  • Poet first: although he is best known for his novels in many countries, he thought of himself mainly as a poet. Poems like "Bluebird" and "So You Want to Be a Writer?" are among his most quoted.
  • Famous TV appearance: in 1978 he appeared drunk on a French literary TV show, Apostrophes, and walked off in the middle of it, which made him even more famous in France.
